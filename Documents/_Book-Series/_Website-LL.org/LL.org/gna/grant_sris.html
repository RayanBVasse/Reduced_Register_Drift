GNA Research · Prototype 4

Grant, Franklin & Langford — Self-Reflection and Insight (SRIS)

Self-reflection and insight are distinct capacities with opposite mental health correlates. The SRIS — published in 2002 and cited over 660 times — is the standard instrument for both. GNA’s Prototype 4 extends the SRIS into conversational text: eliciting self-reflective processing and identifying whether it produces insight or rumination from the language it leaves behind.

§ 01 · The problem

§ 01 · The problem this prototype addresses

High self-reflection without insight predicts anxiety, not wellbeing. No text-based measure of the difference exists.

The SRIS establishes a critical asymmetry: self-reflection (the tendency to inspect one’s own thoughts, feelings, and behaviour) correlates positively with anxiety and stress. Insight (the clarity of understanding that inspection produces) correlates negatively with depression, anxiety, and alexithymia, and positively with cognitive flexibility and self-regulation. These are independent factors, not a single dimension.

This asymmetry has significant implications for any system designed to support self-development: prompting people to examine themselves without facilitating the SR-to-IN conversion may increase ruminative self-focus rather than genuine understanding. Yet no text-based or conversational measure exists that can detect whether self-reflective processing is producing insight or rumination. GNA’s Prototype 4 is the first attempt to build one.

§ 02 · Theoretical foundation

§ 02 · Theoretical foundation

Layer A — The foundational paper

Grant, Franklin & Langford (2002): the SRIS comprises two separate factors — Self-Reflection (SRIS-SR) and Insight (SRIS-IN). SRIS-SR correlates positively with anxiety and stress. SRIS-IN is negatively correlated with depression, anxiety, stress, and alexithymia, and positively correlated with cognitive flexibility and self-regulation. Test–retest reliability over 7 weeks: .77 (SR) and .78 (IN). Social Behavior and Personality, 30(8), 821–836.

Layer B — The SR / IN asymmetry replicated

Harrington & Loffredo (2010): insight, but not self-reflection, is related to subjective wellbeing. High SR scores are associated with neuroticism, lower self-esteem, higher trait anxiety, excessive rumination, and social anxiety. High insight shows the inverse pattern across all of these. The Journal of Psychology, 145(1), 39–57.

Adaptive self-reflection and resilience (2021): insight mediates the relationship between adaptive self-reflection and resilience. Rumination moderates this: when rumination is low, self-reflection leads to insight and then to resilience. When rumination is high, the SR-to-IN pathway is disrupted. Personality and Individual Differences.

Layer C — The coaching psychology context

Grant (2003): adults completing a life coaching programme targeting long-held unresolved goals showed enhanced mental health, quality of life, and goal attainment. Levels of self-reflection decreased but insight increased. Effective development does not make people more self-reflective — it makes them more insightful. Social Behavior and Personality, 31, 253–264.

Layer D — The diary paradox

From the original SRIS validation: individuals who had kept diaries had higher SRIS-SR scores but lower SRIS-IN scores than those who had not. Journalling increases self-reflection but not necessarily insight. This finding directly constrains GNA’s design: the AI-driven elicitation must not simply prompt for more self-examination — it must actively facilitate the SR-to-IN conversion.

Layer E — Psychometric maturity

Silvia (2021) — 12-item short form: IRT analysis produced a concise scale, evenly balanced between SR and IN items, with strong dimensionality, reliability, and essentially no gender differential item functioning. Current Psychology, 41, 8635–8645.

Banner et al. (2024): SRIS validated with registered psychologists (n = 123). The scale has been validated across medical students, nurses, individuals with depression, young people in therapy, and multiple languages.

§ 03 · What we built

§ 03 · What we built

An AI-driven elicitation tool that operationalises the SR / IN distinction in conversational text.

GNA’s Prototype 4 extends the SRIS from self-report into conversational text. The tool elicits self-reflective processing through Grant’s framework, then examines whether that processing produces insight-generating language or ruminative language — as defined by the textual signatures that distinguish the two in the SRIS literature.

The design principle is derived directly from Grant (2003): every reflective prompt (which opens self-examination) is paired with a clarity prompt (which closes it toward understanding rather than leaving it open to circulate). The session does not aim to maximise self-reflection — it aims to maximise the SR-to-IN conversion.

Ruminative language is characterised by: circular return to the same material without progression; unresolved questions about the self without movement toward understanding; repeated attribution of the same pattern to the same obstacle without new insight. Insight-generating language is characterised by: explicit self-understanding statements (‘I realise now’, ‘I understand that I’); pattern recognition (‘whenever X, I tend to Y’); causal self-knowledge (‘I do this because’); revised self-concept (‘I used to think I was… but actually’).

The session produces a narrative record scored for the presence and quality of these two language patterns — providing a text-based correlate of the SR / IN profile the SRIS captures through self-report.

§ 04 · The SRIS

§ 04 · The SRIS (validation benchmark)

Published: Grant, A.M., Franklin, J., & Langford, P. (2002). Social Behavior and Personality, 30(8), 821–836.

The SRIS is a 20-item scale measuring individual differences in self-reflection and insight. A validated 12-item short form (Silvia 2021) is evenly balanced between SR and IN items. GNA administers the 12-item short form as part of the session and uses scores as the validation benchmark for text-derived SR / IN profiles.

Sample SR items: “I frequently examine my feelings” — “I’m always trying to figure myself out.”

Sample IN items: “I usually know why I feel the way I do” — “I have a clear idea of what my strengths and weaknesses are.”

Full scale: Grant et al. (2002). Short form: Silvia (2021). Items are not reproduced in full here.

§ 05 · Elicitation protocol

§ 05 · The AI-driven elicitation protocol

Five blocks — each reflective prompt paired with a clarity prompt — about 15 minutes total.

  • Block 1 — Current reflection: “When something is weighing on you, what do you tend to do with it internally? Does thinking about it usually help, or does it tend to go in circles?” (Directly probes the SR / IN distinction.)
  • Block 2 — Pattern recognition: “Is there something you notice about yourself that keeps recurring? What do you understand about where that comes from?”
  • Block 3 — Change and learning: “Think of a time you genuinely understood something about yourself that you hadn’t before. What produced that understanding?”
  • Block 4 — Limits of self-knowledge: “Is there something about yourself you’re still trying to understand — that reflection hasn’t fully resolved?” (Honest about the limits — not every question needs a resolution.)
  • Block 5 — Integration: “What do you know about yourself now that you didn’t five years ago? And how did you come to know it?”

Governance constraint: when a participant’s responses show the circular ruminative pattern (returning to the same material without development), the AI-driven interviewer redirects toward clarity rather than deepening the self-examination. This is the critical distinction between a self-development tool and a rumination amplifier.

§ 06 · Validation materials

§ 06 · Validation materials

Gutenberg corpus — 5 books

  • Autobiography of John Stuart Mill — explicit SR-to-IN arc in the mental crisis account; high insight in the Wordsworth passage.
  • The Confessions of St. Augustine — complex SR throughout, sustained high IN in the later books.
  • Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin — predominantly descriptive; low SR, low IN; useful as low-end anchor.
  • Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass — high IN throughout: pattern recognition, causal self-knowledge, integrated understanding of systemic forces.
  • The Story of My Life — Helen Keller — moderate SR, high IN; clear integration of reflection into understanding and action.

Corpus analysis in progress. Passage selection, scoring, and inter-rater reliability results will be reported here on completion.

SRIS short form — validation benchmark

Participants completing the GNA session also complete the 12-item SRIS short form (Silvia 2021). The proposed finding: text-derived SR / IN profile scores correlate with self-report SRIS scores — establishing a text-based correlate of the SR / IN distinction for the first time.

§ 07 · What GNA offers

§ 07 · What GNA offers Grant’s research programme

A text-based correlate of the SR / IN distinction — extending the SRIS into conversational assessment.

  • A text-based correlate of the SR / IN distinction — the first such measure, validated against the SRIS short form.
  • Dynamic SR-to-IN tracking within a single session — does self-reflection convert to insight during the conversation itself?
  • An integration architecture: the SR / IN profile is the quality-control layer over narrative identity (Prototype 2), emotional granularity (Prototype 1), and experiential richness (Prototype 3) — determining whether reflection on all three produces genuine self-understanding or ruminative re-circulation.
  • Proposed collaboration: participants complete the AI-driven elicitation tool and the SRIS short form; text-derived SR / IN scores are correlated with self-report scores; the finding — whether conversational text captures the same SR / IN distinction as the Likert scale — is the publication.
